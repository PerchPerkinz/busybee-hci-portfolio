Busy Bee

A crowdsourced, real-time app that shows how busy a place is right now — before you walk in.

Why this project

Deciding whether a coffee shop, gym, or restaurant is worth the trip usually means guessing. Busy Bee lets people report and see live crowd levels at nearby venues, so the guess becomes a quick check instead. Our team is using it as the foundation for this semester's HCI design process, working through research, prototyping, and evaluation with real users in mind.

Phase 1

Project Description

In progress

What Busy Bee is, who it is for, and how we intend to build it over the semester.

Problem statement

There is no reliable way to find out how busy a place is right now. The tools people have describe the past: Google's popular times is a historical average that cannot know it is midterms week, posted wait times go stale without saying so, and reviews describe a visit that happened months ago. So people guess. They pad their schedules, take their chances, or drive somewhere and turn around in the parking lot.

Busy Bee closes that gap the way Waze closed it for traffic. People already inside a place submit a two-second report, and those reports aggregate into a live busyness score anyone can check before they leave. Reports expire after 30 minutes, so the score always describes the present rather than a pattern. It works for any physical establishment — restaurants, cafes, gyms, stores, airport security lines — because the underlying problem is the same everywhere: the only person who really knows what it is like inside is someone standing in it.

Target users

Anyone whose plans change based on how crowded somewhere is — which, in our Phase 2 research, turned out to be everyone we interviewed, for very different reasons. Three groups emerged from that data.

People under time pressure, where being wrong is expensive rather than annoying — a security line before a flight, an errand squeezed between commitments. They currently manage the risk by padding their schedule, sometimes by 45 minutes.

Spontaneous, socially-driven users, mostly students, who decide where to go while already walking and coordinate in group chats. A full cafe means a wasted trip; a dead bar on a Friday night means the wrong choice. They need busyness read in both directions.

Crowd-averse and family users, who avoid crowds rather than endure them. With young children along, any wait at all can end the outing.

Today all three do the same things instead: check Google's historical graph and distrust it, text someone already there, call the venue, or simply show up and find out.

Goals & objectives

By the end of the semester we intend to have a working mobile app, validated against real users, that does four things.

Answer the question before departure. Our research found the decision window closes the moment someone sets off, so the live score has to be readable in seconds, with its own age shown next to it.

Make reporting effectively free. One tap, under two seconds, no typing required — because participants told us plainly that anything slower gets skipped.

Earn trust explicitly. Not one participant said they would trust a crowdsourced app without conditions. Timestamps, reporter counts, decay and a plain-language moderation policy are therefore features, not polish.

Avoid the empty map. An app with nobody posting is worse than no app at all, so seeding venues and concentrating early users geographically matters as much as the interface does.

Approach

We are following the standard HCI design process across seven phases, documented on this site as we go.

Research — contextual inquiry interviews with real participants, coded into an affinity diagram and carried forward into personas (Phase 2). Ideation — task analysis and requirements drawn from those findings (Phase 3). Prototyping — low-fidelity wireframes tested cheaply and revised, then a high-fidelity interactive build in React Native and Expo (Phases 4 and 5). Evaluation — usability testing against the tasks identified in Phase 3, with findings fed back into the design (Phase 6). Reflection — what the process changed about the product, and what we would do differently (Phase 7).

Each phase produces an artifact that lives on this portfolio rather than in a folder, so the reasoning behind the final design stays visible and traceable back to the people we interviewed.

Method

Each team member ran structured interviews using the same ten questions. Seven interviews were completed: Omar Naguib (P1, P2), Evan Wilkin (P3–P5) and Nolan Jarvinen (P6, P7). Every response was broken into single-observation notes and grouped bottom-up following Beyer & Holtzblatt's contextual design method, with group labels phrased in the participants' own voice rather than ours.

P1, P2, P6 and P7 were captured verbatim. P3, P4 and P5 were recorded as interviewer summaries and are marked as such throughout, so the strength of the evidence behind any single note stays visible.

The nine themes

T1

How much busyness matters is set by the trip, not by the person

4 group labels · 24 notes

Design implication: Busyness is not one number. A full cafe is a failed trip for a student and a good sign for a Friday night out. Let a report carry the venue type and let the user state which direction they want — BusyBee's vibe tags are the hook for this, and the gauge should read against the user's intent, not an absolute scale.

T2

The tools I have describe last Tuesday, not today

3 group labels · 12 notes

Design implication: Every score needs a visible age. The competitor is a historical average, so BusyBee wins by never showing one — live-only reports with a 30-minute decay, and a timestamp shown as prominently as the score itself.

T3

A person who is standing there beats any app

4 group labels · 8 notes

Design implication: Frame a report as eyewitness testimony, not prediction. Lead with the human fact — “4 people reported from here in the last 12 minutes” — and keep the aggregation logic out of the way. The product BusyBee is replacing is a text message from a friend.

T4

With no information, I absorb the risk myself

5 group labels · 22 notes

Design implication: The behaviour BusyBee replaces is padding and gambling, not searching. Success is a trip not wasted — so the confirmation screen should tell the reporter what their report prevented, and the app should be measured in avoided trips rather than sessions.

T5

Being wrong costs composure, not just minutes

4 group labels · 13 notes

Design implication: Tone must scale with stakes. A restaurant deserves the calm half-circle gauge; a high-consequence context deserves unambiguous lead-time language and a bias toward over-warning. And because people blame themselves for a wasted trip, the empty state should say the data isn't there yet — never imply the user missed something.

T6

Everyone has a walk-away number, and everyone's is different

5 group labels · 12 notes

Design implication: The walk-away number spans 0 to 45 minutes across seven people. A single “Busy” badge can't serve that range — let users set a personal threshold and alert against it, and support a capacity state (“no tables”) alongside a wait in minutes, because for study trips the wait is irrelevant.

T7

The only useful moment is before I leave, and I'm already moving

6 group labels · 21 notes

Design implication: Two moments carry the product: pre-departure and on arrival. Reading must work before leaving the house; reporting must be a single tap from a notification or widget in under two seconds, with the slider, tags and note all strictly optional.

T8

People already warn each other — they just don't call it reporting

5 group labels · 10 notes

Design implication: The reporting behaviour exists — it's just trapped in group chats. Two moves unlock it: give a report a share-to-chat path so posting publicly costs nothing extra, and close the loop with a reciprocity receipt (“you kept 12 people out of that line”) rather than abstract points, because “what am I getting out of it” is the real objection.

T9

Trust is conditional — not one participant said yes outright

6 group labels · 15 notes

Design implication: Trust signals are features, not polish. Ship the timestamp, the reporter count and the decay on the face of the score; write a plain-language page on how false reports are handled and link it from the gauge; and seed venues so no one's first open is an empty map. Name Waze and GasBuddy in onboarding — two participants reached for them unprompted.

What surprised us

Nobody said an unqualified yes

Every single participant attached a condition to trusting a crowdsourced app — a timestamp, a reporter count, a moderation policy, an existing community, or a two-week trial. Trust signals are not polish here; they are the product.

Walk-away times span 0 to 45 minutes

P3 leaves immediately with two young children in tow; P7 will wait 45 minutes if he is hungry enough. A single “Busy” badge cannot serve that range, which argues for a user-set threshold.

Busy is not always bad

P2 wants a cafe empty for studying and a bar full on a Friday night. Busyness has a direction, and it changes with intent rather than with the venue.

People already report — privately

Participants text friends constantly but would not post publicly. P1: “Who's reading it? What am I getting out of it?” The behaviour exists; the payoff is what is missing.
